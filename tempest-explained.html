The squash that came out of a radio

Angus Young's crunch was shaped by a 1970s wireless transmitter that was never meant to touch his tone. The Storm pedal, and Tempest after it, keep the useful accident: a boost with a light bulb inside it. Move the dials below and watch what it does to a riff.

An accident on a stadium stage

In the mid-1970s, Ken Schaffer built a wireless system so guitarists could leave the cable behind. Radio links of the day were noisy, so he did what tape machines did: squeezed the signal in the transmitter and expanded it back out in the receiver. That compander gave roughly 100 dB of dynamic range against about 70 for its rivals.

The two halves never quite cancelled. Fast pick attacks came out the far end shaped and pushed, and the receiver's output stage was strong enough to hit an amplifier hard. Players heard their guitar get tighter, louder and longer, and kept the pack on their belt for the sound.

  1. 1976

    The Schaffer-Vega Diversity System arrives: transmitter, receiver and a compander between them, with an optical limiter ahead of it in the transmitter.

  2. 1977

    Angus Young starts using it, from the Powerage era on. It boosts his Marshalls into the crunch that defines the records that follow.

  3. 2010s

    SoloDallas reverse-engineers the circuit with Ken Schaffer and releases the Schaffer Replica, a full-size unit with the compander and EQ.

  4. 2016

    The Storm puts the sound in a small pedal. The first version's knobs are Power, Storm and Snap.

  5. 2019

    Version 2 renames them Gain, Boost and Limiter. This is the unit DIY makers traced, and the one Tempest models.

  6. 2026

    A third version moves the jacks to the top and describes its boost as LM308-flavoured, which may mean a changed circuit.

What's actually in the box

The marketing talks about companders and expanders, but a traced 2019 Storm is simpler than that, and the simplicity is the point. There are four stages, and the trick is how two of them talk to each other.

The op-amp stage amplifies your guitar up to 8.5 times. Across its feedback resistor sits a light-dependent resistor sealed in a tube with an LED. Light it up and the resistance falls, and so does the gain: down to about 1.1 times. That's up to 18 dB of squeeze, built into the amplifier itself rather than added after it.

The sidechain takes the op-amp's output, multiplies it by up to 10 depending on the Limiter knob, rectifies it and drives the LED. The louder you play, the brighter the LED, the lower the gain.

The LM386 is a tiny power-amp chip, more at home in a practice amp than a studio. It multiplies by 20 and runs out of room early, folding the top of the wave over softly and a little unevenly. That's the crunch the pedal makes on its own.

Boost is just a volume control at the very end. It sets how hard you hit the amp and never changes the pedal's own drive.

There's no tone circuit at all. Apart from its coupling capacitors the pedal is flat from about 40 Hz to beyond 20 kHz. The weight and smoothness players describe comes from dynamics and harmonics, not EQ.

Why it feels so good under your fingers

Follow one hard-picked chord through the pedal and the "controlled, squishy crunch" falls out of the timing.

The first 4 milliseconds

The pick gets through

The light cell needs a few milliseconds to react, so the very front of each pick attack gets through before the squeeze arrives. You keep the snap and definition of each hit; it never sounds like a blanket thrown over the guitar.

The next half second

The body gets held level

Then the LED lights and the gain drops just enough to hold the chord at a steady level. Because the limiter listens to its own output, it backs off as it works: the harder it squeezes, the less it wants to. That gives a gentle, rounded curve rather than a hard ceiling, which is where the "squishy" comes from.

All the time

The clipping stays on a leash

The LM386 starts clipping once the op-amp passes about 1.1 V. Turn the Limiter up and the op-amp is held below that line, so the chip saturates gently instead of buzzing. Gain decides how much crunch the pedal makes; Limiter decides how much of it you're allowed. That's the "controlled" part.

After you stop

The note blooms back

Light cells let go slowly, and more slowly the longer they've been lit. As a chord rings out, the squeeze eases off over a few hundred milliseconds and the gain climbs back up, so the decaying note is lifted. Chords hang in the air longer, and that's the sustain.

At the amp

Your amp gets an even shove

Here's why it works so well into an amp on the edge of breakup, like your Bella. The pedal hands it a signal that's up to 28 dB hotter and far more even than your guitar's. The amp's own breakup is then the same from hit to hit: every chord pushes it the same distance past clean. Ease off your guitar's volume and the whole chain cleans up together.

Before any of it

Your pickups get tamed

The pedal's input is about 100k, a tenth of a typical amp's. That damps the bright resonant peak of a passive pickup by around 10 dB, taking the fizz off the top before any distortion happens. It's a subtle part of why the crunch sounds warm rather than spiky.

Every dial and switch

The three knobs and footswitch on the pedal:

Gain 0 to 10

In the circuit
A 100k volume pot before the op-amp. It sets how much signal reaches both the light cell and the LM386.
What you hear
Low: clean and open. Middle: the limiter starts to breathe. High: the LM386 adds its own grit, and the limiter has the most to work with.
Tip
The Limiter does very little with Gain low. Raise them together.

Limiter 0 to 10

In the circuit
Sets the sidechain's gain from 0 to 10 on a log taper, so most of the action is in the top half of its travel.
What you hear
0: off, unless you hit it very hard with Gain full. 5: chords even out and sustain longer. 10: up to 15 dB of squash, thick and singing.
Tip
Watch the Opto window: a gentle glow on each chord is the sweet spot for rhythm.

Boost 0 to 10

In the circuit
A plain 1k volume pot after the LM386.
What you hear
Only level. It sets how hard you hit the amp, so it decides how much the amp breaks up, not the pedal.
Tip
Into an amp sim, 3 to 5 is often plenty. Full Boost is about 28 dB above your guitar.

Engage footswitch

In the circuit
True bypass. Off returns your guitar untouched, with a short crossfade so it never clicks.
What you hear
The blue light means on. The Opto window shows the squeeze; the Clip window flashes when the LM386 runs out of room.

Inside the box

The green board under the pedal holds the things you'd set once with a screwdriver.

Supply 9 V or 12 V

In the circuit
The pedal runs on either. More volts give both chips more room to swing.
What you hear
12 V clips later and sounds a little cleaner and punchier with Gain high. 9 V crunches sooner.

Iso cap open or fitted

In the circuit
A 10 µF capacitor across the LED. The board has a place for it, but the traced pedal left it empty.
What you hear
Open (stock): the LED flickers with each wave, adding a little grit to low notes when the Limiter is high. Fitted: smoother and a touch slower to react.

Pickup load none, single coil, humbucker

In the circuit
Adds back the 100k load your pickups would see from the real pedal, which a recording interface's input doesn't provide.
What you hear
Humbucker: the top end softens around 2.5 to 3 kHz. Single coil: the same around 4 kHz. None: for active pickups or re-amped tracks.

Input ref −12 to +12 dBV

In the circuit
How many volts a full-scale digital sample stands for, so your DI hits the modelled circuit at a realistic level.
What you hear
Higher drives the circuit harder, like a hotter pickup. If it's too fizzy or too clean, adjust this before Boost.
